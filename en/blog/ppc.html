PPC: What It Measures and What It Doesn't

A weekly PPC of 90% sounds like a project under control. It might be. It might also mean your crews are only promising what is easy.

Percent Plan Complete (PPC, also written Percent Planned Complete) is the most quoted number in the Last Planner System (LPS). It is also one of the most misread. This note covers what PPC measures, three common misreadings, and what to do with the number once you have it.

What PPC measures

PPC is the share of weekly commitments that were completed as promised:

PPC = commitments completed ÷ commitments made

The commitments are the ones the last planners (foremen and trade leads) made in the weekly work plan. Two rules define the measure:

  • It is binary. A commitment is either complete or not complete. There is no partial credit, because a successor trade cannot start on 95% of a predecessor's scope.
  • It measures promises, not production. PPC does not tell you how much work was done. It tells you how reliable the commitments were.

Illustrative example (numbers invented for explanation): a team makes 12 commitments for the week and 9 are complete. PPC is 9 ÷ 12 = 75%. One of the three misses is a crew that finished everything on a level except a single riser. That is still a miss, because the next trade could not start.

Why measure promise reliability at all? In LPS, the schedule is a forecast, and the people closest to the work make the real weekly promises. Downstream crews plan around those promises. The lean construction literature reports that in traditional practice only about half of the tasks assigned for a given week are completed (Ballard & Howell's measurements, as summarized in Forbes & Ahmed, 2010). When promises are unreliable, every successor trade pays for it.

Binary scoring only works with small tasks

Complete or not complete is fair only when the task is small enough to be checked clearly. If a commitment is "finish the second floor MEP rough-in," someone can always say it is "mostly done," and the conversation turns into an argument about percentages. The team never gets a clean answer.

So the size of the commitment comes first. Break the work down until each commitment is something one foreman can control and a person can verify by looking: "all sprinkler drops in Area A installed," not "sprinklers, second floor." The look-ahead criteria described in the literature point the same way: items should be small enough to be controlled and easy to measure (Forbes & Ahmed, 2010).

Small tasks also make the team conversation easier. If a crew says the task is 90% done, the answer is simple and not hostile: finish it, and then it counts as complete. Nobody is being punished. The task is simply not yet finished for the crews waiting behind it, and the weekly review is exactly where that gets said out loud.

Three ways PPC gets misread

1. A high PPC is read as a healthy project

PPC says how reliable the promises are. It does not say whether the promises were the right ones. A crew that commits only to easy work can score 100% while the project falls behind. This is sometimes called sandbagging.

The aim is promises that are both reliable and ambitious. If PPC is consistently very high and milestones are still slipping, check the look-ahead: are the committed tasks the ones the milestones actually need?

2. PPC becomes a report card

If PPC is used to rank trades, people learn to game it: they promise less, or argue about what counts as complete. It also misleads, because a trade can miss a commitment for reasons outside its control. A drywall crew with labor and materials ready cannot finish if the inspection it depends on was missed.

For this reason, many teams look at PPC at team level rather than as a score per trade. The point of the number is learning, not blame.

3. The score is recorded, the reasons are not

A PPC chart with no reasons behind it is a thermometer with no diagnosis. The useful part of the weekly review is asking, for every missed commitment: why?

Reasons for non-completion

Record one main reason for each miss, chosen from a short, fixed list. A practical starting list (adapt it to your project):

  • Weather
  • Manpower
  • Equipment
  • Design or information
  • Make-ready needs (prerequisite work or inspection not done)
  • Materials
  • Poor scheduling (over-commitment)

After a few weeks, count the reasons. A simple Pareto of the causes usually shows one or two that account for most of the misses, and that is where improvement effort should go.

Notice how this connects to the constraint log. A repeated "make-ready" or "materials" miss usually means constraints were not found and cleared early enough in the look-ahead. PPC reasons are the feedback signal that tells you the make-ready process is leaking.

A simple weekly routine

  1. Size commitments so they can be checked. Small, specific, verifiable by eye.
  2. Score at the end of the week, for example in the Friday huddle. Complete or not complete, no partial credit.
  3. Record one reason per miss from the fixed list.
  4. Act while it is fresh. If a missed commitment was caused by missing design information, the request goes out that day, not at the next monthly meeting.
  5. Review the trend of reasons, not only the percentage.
  6. Feed the findings back into the constraint log and the next look-ahead.

The point is not to chase 100%. The point is a team whose promises can be trusted, because everything downstream depends on them.

Question for planners: what is the most common reason for missed commitments on your sites: make-ready, materials, or over-commitment?
